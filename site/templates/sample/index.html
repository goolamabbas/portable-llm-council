A small-business decision

A profitable contract.
Can the business afford it?

Maya owns a commercial laundry. A hotel group offers reliable volume—but serving it would use more cash and nearly all her peak capacity.

Two councils examined this fictional decision. One ran in Claude Code with Sonnet 5.5, the other in Factory Droid with Gemini 4 Hydrogen. Each used its own five advisors, five fresh reviewers, and chair.

The decision

More revenue brings new obligations.

The full hotel offer lasts 26 weeks. A smaller, 13-week allocation could leave more breathing room, but the hotel has not accepted it. Declining preserves capacity for existing customers and two uncommitted prospects.

Maya starts with $22,000 cash and wants at least $10,000 at every week-end. Hotel invoices are paid four weeks after service; the brief also asks what happens if each payment is two weeks late.

Both councils’ recommendation

Ask for the smaller deal. Keep a conditional fallback.

Use two hours to call the hotel and send the smaller proposal. Ask for an answer by September 6, two days before the full offer expires. Accept the full contract only with agreed payment protection and a workable plan for weeks 14–26. Otherwise decline.

Before signing: the hotel must accept the relevant terms, and capacity must cover the commitment. A proposed deposit or faster payment is a negotiation request, not money already available.

Where did the two councils differ?

Claude Code with Sonnet 5.5. Tell the hotel up front what would make the full contract workable. The hotel can consider the smaller proposal and Maya’s fallback conditions in the same conversation. Reviewers also corrected two figures the chair relied on: the smaller contract’s cash low under late payment and its spare capacity in an ordinary week.

Factory Droid with Gemini 4 Hydrogen. Lead with the smaller proposal and keep the fallback terms ready until the hotel replies. One reviewer also suggested calling two prospective customers before signing. If both commit from week one, they could earn Maya just $205 less over 13 weeks than the smaller hotel deal, with payment the same week.

Maya knows the hotel relationship. She can decide whether naming her fallback early would help the negotiation.

What the publication check found

Reviewers caught mistakes in the cash and capacity estimates. Four criticized the Outsider partly for giving no recommendation, although that advisor’s job was to identify unclear assumptions. The chair incorrectly described the criticism as unanimous.

The $1,800 cash shortfall is the minimum relief needed under the specified delay. A deposit’s value depends on when Maya receives it and how it is credited against invoices. The original audit also misstated one payment term. The chair received a corrected packet after the first attempt altered an advisor’s wording.

What the verification found

All five reviewers received the original answers in the intended orders, and the chair received every answer and review. The original export contains an incorrect task ID for the Expansionist. Its answer matches the worker record.

The report groups two changes to the full contract in one row. Faster payment changes cash timing. A flat weekly volume of 2,500 lb changes profit to $9,800 and still leaves cash $400 below Maya’s floor in the delay scenario. The $1,800 shortfall on the original volumes also requires explicit deposit and invoice terms.

What can you take from this example?

Maya gave the councils a number that mattered to her: she needed to keep $10,000 in the bank. That let her see when a profitable offer could leave her short of cash. For your own decision, what limit would make you turn down an otherwise attractive option?

These were two runs on a fictional brief. The assistants, models, reasoning settings, and assignment versions differed. The records show how the method worked in each setting. The business outcome remains hypothetical.
