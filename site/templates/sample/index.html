Workshop first.

A solo consultant wants a repeatable training offer. Should they teach a paid live workshop within three weeks, or spend six weeks building a recorded course?

The brief

Interest is there.
Paid demand is unknown.

The goal: at least $3,000 in monthly training revenue within six months, while protecting existing client work.

The consultant has an 800-person email list. Twelve survey respondents expressed interest, and three existing clients informally encouraged offering training. Nobody has paid, placed a deposit, or committed to buy.

Capacity is eight hours a week for six weeks, with up to $1,500 available before sales.

Compare the two options

Live workshop: $150 per seat, 10–15 participants, estimated 22 total hours and $300 direct spending. Repeat sessions require live attendance.

Self-paced course: $120 per purchase, estimated 42 hours and $1,200 direct spending. Ongoing support is estimated at two hours a week; that estimate is untested.

All figures are hypothetical preliminary estimates and may be optimistic.

Chair’s recommendation · editorial summary

Pre-sell a tightly scoped live pilot.

Deliver if ten people pay $150 by a firm cutoff and the work still fits the available time. Delay course production until repeat demand and a manageable delivery workload are demonstrated.

Why: a pilot exposes less money and tests willingness to pay sooner. One successful cohort does not establish recurring revenue.

A full workshop grosses $2,250. Reaching $3,000 requires 20 seats a month at $150, or 25 course purchases at $120. Neither volume is established.

Where the method helped

What did the reviews add?

01

Look beyond the first cohort

Warm-list demand may not repeat. Reviewers asked for evidence of acquiring another cohort of buyers.

02

Diagnose weak enrollment

A low sales count alone cannot distinguish poor reach from an unclear promise, price, timing, or lack of demand.

03

Keep the offer focused

Team licences, templates, and advisory packages were possibilities. Reviewers questioned expanding before the core training offer had been tested.

Where the advisors differed

Some proposed eight to ten buyers; one considered four to seven for learning value; others required ten. The chair chose ten and retained a requirement to protect preparation time.

What the chair took forward

Track purchase sources, test a second cohort of mostly new buyers, and require useful participant outcomes before investing in a course.

One paid cohort is evidence of initial demand. It is not yet evidence of repeatable monthly revenue.
The first step · editorial summary

Put a concrete offer in front of buyers.

Define the three workflows, the outcomes, a date, the $150 price, a 15-seat cap, and a ten-person minimum. Offer a seven-day paid presale with clear refund terms.

The chair suggests doing this in the next week’s eight hours. The review below identifies remaining questions about the preparation schedule and handling a revised presale.

Read the result critically

A useful example, with visible limits.

This run illustrates the workflow. It is not a forecast or proof that the recommendation is correct. A later editorial review identified assumptions and unresolved execution details.

The email-funnel estimate assumes more than the brief establishes
The 32-click illustration assumes delivery to all 800 subscribers. Equating clicks with distinct sales-page visitors adds another assumption. Calling the resulting conversion rate unusually high requires a benchmark that the scenario does not supply.
The three-week schedule still needs to be reconciled
A first week using eight hours on the presale leaves 16 hours before delivery. Whether that is enough depends on how much presale work is included in the 18-hour preparation estimate. The chair permits rescheduling or cancellation but does not supply a reconciled weekly plan. Availability beyond the first six weeks is also unknown.
Refunds and thresholds need explicit operating rules
The promise of automatic refunds below ten buyers needs to be reconciled with a second presale. Refunding the first offer and asking buyers to opt into revised terms is one possible implementation, but was not specified. The ten-buyer threshold and stopping rules are judgments, not validated benchmarks.
What is—and is not—verified about this run
Five advisor returns, five fresh reviewer returns, and a separate chair return are visible in the conversation. Advisors and reviewers ran in batches of three and two. Fresh contexts were requested and model overrides omitted. Effective model identities were unverified; filesystem access was shared. The execution record reconstructs some packets from components, rather than storing an independent raw export. No external research was used.
Editorial preservation note
This page separates editorial summaries from returned analyses. The original transcript reformats headings, and the original HTML omits a short chair lead-in; neither is a byte-exact raw export. Worker packets omitted the final instruction not to assume either option would achieve the revenue goal, although the uncertainty was supplied and outputs did not promise success.
