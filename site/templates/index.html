The method

Five advisors, five blind reviews, one chair.

Each round has a different job: explore, challenge, then decide.

01

Explore the options

Five separate advisors receive the same brief. Each uses a different lens to test assumptions, objectives, upside, clarity, or feasibility.

02

Challenge the arguments

Five fresh reviewers each read the brief and all five answers. They see neither the advisors’ names nor the other reviewers’ judgments.

What do the reviewers look for?
03

Choose a next step

A separate chair weighs the answers and reviews, explains disagreements, and recommends a course of action. Strong reasoning can outweigh the majority.

Contrarian

Which assumption is most vulnerable?

Tests the assumption the plan depends on most and explains what happens if it fails.

First Principles

What are we actually trying to achieve?

Starts from the owner’s objective and asks whether the original question frames the right decision.

Expansionist

What upside are we overlooking?

Looks for an overlooked opportunity and identifies what would have to be true to capture it.

Outsider

What is unclear or taken for granted?

Spots jargon and claims that assume too much, then explains what the owner should clarify before deciding.

Executor

What can we do with the resources available?

Works out what has to happen first, who or what it depends on, and whether it can happen in time.

What do the reviewers look for?

Each reviewer answers the same three questions.

  1. Which answer makes the strongest case, and why?
  2. Which answer has the biggest blind spot, and what did it overlook?
  3. Looking across all five answers, is there anything important that every advisor missed?

A reviewer can say the five answers covered the important ground.

Why change the reading order?

Even when the authors are anonymous, changing the order of the same answers can change which one an AI reviewer finds strongest. All five council reviewers see the same answers and answer the same three questions. We rotate the answers so each appears once in every position, giving every argument an equal turn at going first, last, and somewhere in between. For example, the first reviewer reads A, B, C, D, E. The next reads B, C, D, E, A. Each letter still refers to the same answer.

From discussion to action

You get a report and a full transcript.

See what the council recommends, why it recommends it, and where the advisors disagree.

Use the report to question the assumptions behind the recommendation, identify what still needs checking, and decide whether the proposed next step fits your circumstances.

In the ClearFold Laundry example, reviewers caught errors in one advisor’s cash estimate and another’s capacity estimate. The chair used the corrected figures. See what peer review changed for ClearFold.

If a disagreement could change your decision, use the transcript to follow the chair’s account back to the review and the advisor’s original answer.

When to use it

Worth it for decisions that are costly to reverse.

The council is intended for consequential business and technical decisions: product direction, pricing, hiring, positioning, or an implementation strategy.

Give it your objective, alternatives, constraints, evidence, and important unknowns. Those details give each perspective something concrete to examine.

For routine questions, a direct answer may be enough. When a small experiment can resolve the uncertainty, that experiment may be the more useful next step.

Keep the limits in view

Different perspectives do not create independent factual evidence. Agreement does not establish truth, and anonymous labels cannot conceal every stylistic clue.

A full council uses 11 agent runs, which can take more time and tokens than a direct answer. Results depend on the model, the decision brief, and the AI assistant’s ability to maintain separate worker contexts. If a required round cannot finish, the result should be marked incomplete.
