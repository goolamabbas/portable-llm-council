# Paid live workshop vs. self-paced course

> **Fictional scenario; actual council run.**

Run timestamp: `20260920T213624+0800`  
Status: Complete — five advisors, five fresh blind reviewers, one separate chair.

## Original question

Should a small consultancy launch a paid live workshop within three weeks, or spend six weeks developing a self-paced course first? The council may recommend a staged approach or neither option if the evidence supports it.

## Complete scenario and decision brief

### Business and objective

A solo consultant helps small professional-services businesses improve recurring administrative workflows. Client work currently pays the bills, but revenue depends heavily on the consultant's time.

The objective is to develop a repeatable training offer that can generate at least $3,000 in monthly revenue within six months, while limiting financial exposure and protecting existing client commitments. Revenue is before expenses and tax.

The proposed topic is: “Use AI to streamline three recurring administrative workflows.” The consultant has experience delivering this work for clients but has never sold a public workshop or self-paced course.

### Available resources

- A maximum of $1,500 can be spent before the first sales.
- Eight hours per week are available for the next six weeks.
- Existing client work cannot be reduced during that period.
- The consultant already has basic video-call, payment, and email tools.
- There is no employee or contractor available to help.

### Audience and evidence

- An email list has 800 subscribers, mostly small-business owners and operations staff.
- Recent newsletters received approximately 40% opens and 4% link clicks, measured against delivered emails.
- A recent survey received 24 responses. Twelve respondents expressed interest in training on this topic.
- Five respondents preferred live teaching, four preferred recordings, and three had no preference.
- Three existing clients have informally said the consultant should offer training.
- No one has been asked to pay, placed a deposit, or committed to purchase.
- No paid acquisition campaign has been tested.
- The survey may overrepresent the most engaged subscribers; broader demand is unknown.

### Option A: Paid live workshop

- A two-hour workshop priced at $150 per participant.
- Target attendance of 10–15 participants, with a maximum of 15.
- Estimated preparation and administration: 18 hours before delivery.
- Estimated direct spending: $300.
- Delivery plus follow-up: approximately four hours.
- Participants would work through examples and have opportunities to ask questions.
- A recording could be provided to attendees, but would require further editing and permission checks before becoming a separately sold product.
- Repeating the workshop would require the consultant's live attendance.

### Option B: Self-paced course

- A recorded course priced at $120 per purchaser.
- Estimated production and launch work: 42 hours over six weeks.
- Estimated direct spending: $1,200.
- Expected ongoing support: two hours per week at modest sales volume; this estimate has not been tested.
- The product could be purchased without coordinating attendance.
- The consultant has not established a sales conversion rate, completion rate, or likely refund rate.
- Changes to AI tools could require updates to the material.

All time and cost estimates are the consultant's preliminary estimates and may be optimistic.

### Preferences and constraints

The consultant values reusable work and a manageable schedule, but is willing to teach live if it materially improves learning about customer demand.

Avoid relying on aggressive sales tactics, untested advertising returns, or an assumed conversion from survey interest to purchases.

The recommendation should address:

- Which option, sequence, or alternative best fits the objective and constraints.
- What evidence would most change that recommendation.
- What would justify proceeding, changing direction, or stopping.
- One concrete first step that fits within the next week's eight-hour allowance.

### Sources and evidence boundary

### Contrarian

The most vulnerable assumption is not production time or format preference; it is willingness to pay. Twelve survey expressions—possibly from unusually engaged subscribers—cannot support either a 42-hour course investment or an expectation of $3,000 monthly revenue. At the stated prices, that target requires 20 workshop seats or 25 course purchases every month, before accounting for refunds, support, or audience exhaustion.

Do not build the self-paced course first. Its apparent scalability could conceal the fatal flaw: producing reusable material for demand that has never been commercially validated. Instead, stage a paid live pilot within three weeks, conditional on advance sales. Proceed only if at least 8–10 people pay the full $150 by a fixed go/no-go date; otherwise cancel and refund. Keep spending materially below the $300 estimate until that threshold is reached.

Even a successful pilot would validate only one cohort, not sustained monthly demand. Proceed toward repetition or a recorded product only if attendees complete the workshop, report that the three workflows solve meaningful problems, and a credible share requests follow-on material or permits use of the recording. Change direction if buyers mainly value individualized advice, because that would undermine scalability. Stop this offer if two bounded invitations to the list and existing clients fail to produce the enrollment threshold without discounts or aggressive selling.

Within next week’s eight hours, create a one-page offer and payment page, set the pilot date and refund terms, then send a straightforward invitation to the 12 interested respondents and three supportive clients before approaching the full list.

### First Principles Thinker

The decision is not really “live versus recorded.” It is how to validate paid demand and create reusable training without jeopardizing client revenue. On that basis, producing the self-paced course first is premature: it consumes nearly the full six-week capacity and most of the budget before any purchase evidence exists.

Use a staged, paid-validation approach. Pre-sell one tightly scoped live pilot, then build a course only if the pilot demonstrates both demand and repeatable learning needs. At $150, a full workshop produces $2,250; reaching $3,000 monthly would require at least two sessions or a later product that sells without live delivery. The workshop is therefore a learning vehicle, not yet the final business model.

Proceed with delivery if roughly 8–10 people make real, preferably refundable, payments by a stated deadline. That would provide stronger evidence than survey responses while covering direct costs. If fewer than five purchase, pause production and interview buyers and non-buyers about topic, price, timing, and promised outcome. Results between those thresholds justify adjusting the offer and testing once more, not automatically building the course.

The evidence most likely to change the recommendation is actual paid conversion from the email list, followed by participant outcomes, refund requests, recurring questions, and willingness to buy follow-on training. A course becomes justified when the pilot reveals stable material, credible outcomes, and enough demand to make 25 monthly purchases at $120 plausible.

Within the next eight hours, create a one-page pilot offer with three concrete outcomes, date, price, 15-seat cap, refund terms, and payment link, then send it to the list and measure purchases—not clicks or stated interest.

### Expansionist

Launch a paid live workshop first, structured as the first stage of a reusable product rather than a standalone event. If it fills 15 seats, it produces $2,250 revenue on roughly $300 direct spending while revealing buyer objections, useful examples, support needs, and willingness to pay. More importantly, better-than-expected demand could support a broader offer ladder: repeated workshops, team licenses, a refined self-paced course, implementation templates, or higher-priced advisory packages.

The overlooked upside is that live delivery may create the raw material and confidence for a stronger course without committing 42 hours and $1,200 before payment. Design the workshop slides, exercises, and follow-up resources as reusable course components. Do not assume the attendee recording can simply become a product; record clean instructional segments separately later if demand is validated.

Proceed with delivery if at least 8–10 people pay by a firm deadline, generating $1,200–$1,500. Strong evidence for acceleration would be reaching capacity, a waitlist, requests from businesses for team sessions, or several buyers asking for an asynchronous version. If only 4–7 people pay, consider a smaller pilot only if participants accept the format and the learning value justifies it. Fewer than four paid registrations should trigger refunds or postponement and direct buyer interviews rather than course production.

The evidence most likely to change this recommendation would be paid preorders for the self-paced course substantially exceeding live registrations, or clear scheduling barriers among otherwise willing buyers.

Within next week’s eight hours, create a concise workshop outline and sales page, then email the 12 interested respondents with a payment link and a seven-day early validation deadline.

### Outsider

Neither option is yet convincingly supported by demand evidence. The topic is vague: “AI,” “streamline,” and “three recurring workflows” do not tell a buyer which tasks will improve, what tools or skill level are required, or what measurable result they should expect. The audience also combines owners and operations staff, who may have different needs and purchasing authority.

A staged paid validation is the better fit. Offer one live pilot before producing a course, but only proceed if it reaches a preset threshold—such as 10 fully paid seats at $150 by a clear deadline. If it does not, refund purchasers and investigate whether the problem is the topic, positioning, price, format, or audience. Survey interest and informal encouragement are not substitutes for payment.

The economics also need clearer framing. One full live cohort produces $1,500–$2,250 gross, so reaching $3,000 monthly would require roughly two cohorts per month unless recordings or another offer add revenue. The course would need 25 monthly purchases, while its conversion, refunds, completion, support burden, and update frequency are unknown. Its 42-hour estimate also consumes nearly the entire six-week allowance and 80% of the cash budget.

Evidence that would change the recommendation includes paid conversion, buyer objections, attendance, achieved outcomes, support time, and demand for recordings. Strong pilot results would justify turning the proven material into a course; weak sales or outcomes would justify revising or stopping.

Within next week’s eight hours, define the three exact workflows and promised outcomes, publish a simple paid pilot offer, and email the list with a minimum-enrollment refund condition.

### Executor

Its estimated 22-hour total workload narrowly fits the three-week capacity of 24 hours, costs only $300, and tests willingness to pay before committing most of the six-week capacity and $1,200 to an unvalidated course. Because estimates may be optimistic, scope must remain limited to the three promised workflows.

The workshop alone does not establish the $3,000 monthly target: that requires 20 seat-sales per month, likely across repeated cohorts. Its greater value is generating paid-demand evidence, delivery feedback, common questions, and material that could later inform a self-paced product.

Proceed with the workshop only if at least 10 participants pay by a firm cutoff, producing $1,500 gross. If 6–9 pay, investigate objections and consider a smaller pilot only if preparation can be reduced materially. If fewer than six pay despite a clear offer to the existing audience, stop production and reassess the topic, promise, price, or audience. Do not treat clicks or verbal interest as substitutes for payment.

Strong, timely deposits would reinforce the recommendation. Weak paid conversion would argue against both options. Strong workshop outcomes—especially repeated questions, positive feedback, and requests for recordings—would justify developing a lean course afterward. High support needs or rapidly outdated material would favor repeatable live cohorts instead.

Within next week’s eight hours, create a concise paid offer with a fixed date, three concrete outcomes, a 15-seat cap, and an automatic refund if the 10-person threshold is not met; then send it to the survey respondents, interested clients, and email list.

## Separate chair verdict — complete returned output

### Where the Council Agrees

Do not build the six-week self-paced course first. It would consume 42 of the available 48 hours and $1,200 of the $1,500 pre-sales budget while willingness to pay, completion, refunds, support load, and content-update needs remain unknown. That leaves too little contingency to protect client work if the estimates prove optimistic.

The strongest shared recommendation is a conditionally pre-sold live pilot within three weeks. Its estimated 22 hours narrowly fit the 24-hour allowance, its $300 cost limits exposure, and payment provides better evidence than survey interest. The pilot is a validation instrument, not yet a scalable business model.

The revenue arithmetic is decisive:

- A 15-seat workshop grosses $2,250, so one cohort cannot reach the $3,000 monthly objective.
- At $150, the consultancy needs 20 seat-sales per month—probably two cohorts of about ten.
- At $120, a course needs 25 purchases every month, before refunds, support, and audience exhaustion.

Neither format has yet demonstrated that recurring volume.

### Where the Council Clashes

The main disagreement is what to do with middling demand. Some advisors would deliver with 8–10 buyers; one would consider 4–7 for learning value; others require ten fully paid seats. Given the lack of slack in the 22-hour estimate and the need to protect paying client work, ten full-price payments is the sounder delivery threshold. Four to seven buyers do not justify committing nearly three weeks of scarce capacity without materially shrinking the preparation.

There is also tension over whom to approach first. Testing only the 12 interested respondents creates an unrealistically demanding conversion test and reveals little about broader demand. Sending only to the entire list, however, can obscure whether the problem is reach, positioning, or willingness to pay. The answer is one instrumented campaign: personally invite the 12 respondents and three supportive clients, while also offering the same terms to the broader list and tracking each source separately.

The speculative “offer ladder”—team licences, templates, advisory packages—may eventually have merit, but it should not guide the first decision. It expands scope before the core training offer is commercially validated.

### Blind Spots the Council Caught

The historical funnel makes ten sales challenging. A 4% link-click rate across 800 delivered emails implies roughly 32 visits; ten purchases would require about 31% of those visitors to buy unless the offer materially increases reach. Consequently, a failed presale cannot be interpreted from payment totals alone. Delivery, opens, sales-page visits, checkout starts, and completed payments must be separated so the consultant can distinguish a distribution problem from an offer, price, timing, or demand problem.

A successful first workshop could merely exhaust the most engaged subscribers. The central six-month test is therefore not “Did one cohort sell?” but “Can another cohort of mostly new buyers be acquired at full price without aggressive selling, excessive acquisition cost, or more than eight hours of work per week?”

Other material uncertainties are the vague promise, the mixture of owners and operations staff, optimistic workload estimates, untested support demand, rapidly changing AI tools, and the risk that buyers really want individualized consulting. A workshop recording is not automatically a course; permissions, editing, and cleaner instructional production remain separate work.

This verdict is based solely on the hypothetical scenario. No market claims were externally verified. The worker model settings and filesystem isolation were also not independently verifiable, which limits confidence in process independence but does not change the basic capacity and revenue arithmetic.

### The Recommendation

Run a staged paid-validation process, beginning with a live workshop presale for a delivery date within three weeks. Do not produce the self-paced course now.

Use these gates:

- **Proceed:** Deliver only if ten participants pay the full $150 by a firm cutoff early enough to complete preparation without exceeding eight hours in any week. Keep pre-threshold spending close to zero using existing tools, and cap total pilot spending at $300. If the remaining work no longer fits within the 24-hour three-week allowance, move or cancel the event rather than displacing client work.
- **Change:** If six to nine people pay, do not force the current workshop through. Interview buyers and selected non-buyers, refine one variable—such as audience, promised result, date, or format—and run one bounded second presale with funds remaining refundable. Strong scheduling objections accompanied by paid self-paced preorders would support testing a lean asynchronous product instead.
- **Stop:** If fewer than six buy, or if two clear full-price presale tests fail to reach the delivery threshold, stop investing in this offer. Also stop scaling if participants mainly require individualized advice, outcomes are weak, refunds are material, support exceeds the assumed load, or the work threatens client commitments.
- **Advance beyond the pilot:** Require useful participant outcomes, manageable support, reusable questions and exercises, and a second cohort of mostly new full-price buyers. Two cohorts totalling at least 20 seats in a month would demonstrate a plausible—though still labour-dependent—route to $3,000 gross monthly revenue.
- **Build the course later:** Consider the 42-hour course only after repeat demand is shown, the material is stable, buyers explicitly want asynchronous access, and paid preorders or equivalent evidence make 25 monthly purchases credible. Pilot revenue should fund that stage; the remaining budget should not be committed merely because the first workshop filled.

The evidence that would most improve the recommendation is full-price payment conversion by source, followed by second-cohort acquisition from new buyers. The evidence most likely to reverse it would be substantially stronger paid demand for asynchronous access than live attendance, or proof that live buyers primarily need custom consulting rather than repeatable instruction.

### The One Thing to Do First

Within next week’s eight-hour allowance, launch a seven-day paid presale: define the three exact workflows and measurable outcomes, publish a one-page offer with the $150 price, date, 15-seat cap, ten-person minimum, payment link, and automatic-refund terms, then send the same offer to the 12 interested respondents, three supportive clients, and the broader list with source tracking. Do no substantive course or workshop production until the payment gate is met.
